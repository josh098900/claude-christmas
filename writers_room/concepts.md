# Writers' room: concepts

Three pitches, scored on the brief's 12-point rubric before a single frame exists. These are predicted scores: how well the idea *can* score if it's executed well.

---

## A. SEEN FROM SPACE (the lead)

The first Christmas without Grandma, Grandad's house is the only dark one on the street. Nine-year-old Nell spends December on a secret operation. Everyone, the audience included, assumes it's a Santa trap. It turns out to be the lights, and the moment she has to get them onto a roof she's sent to the neighbours. The switch fails. Grandad, who has done this for forty years, finds the loose bulb and lets her twist it. The house blazes, and you can see it from space.

**Strengths:** a spectacular, wordless payoff (light is the emotion). A built-in misdirect. A clean role for Claude: it helps with the thinking, then points her to people when the job needs hands. The "missing note" score device maps exactly onto the dead bulb.
**Risks:** the "lights for a neighbour" DNA is shared with Tesco Ireland 2023 and British Garden Centres 2025. Our protections are the ★ beats: the Santa-trap misdirect, Claude sending her to people, the dead switch, Grandad finishing it, and the pull-out to space.

## B. THE RECIPE

Grandma's Christmas cake was never written down. Nell tries to rebuild it over December: explosions of flour, a kitchen like a lab. The misdirect is that she seems to be doing a chemistry project. Claude helps her think ("what makes a cake dark and sticky?"), but the missing ingredients live in the neighbours' memories, because each of them was given a slice every year. On Christmas Day Grandad tastes it and closes his eyes.

**Strengths:** it's intimate and sensory, the community is woven in, and the gift is homemade.
**Risks:** taste is invisible, so the climax happens *inside* a mouth, which is weak without words. Food ads are the most crowded corner of UK Christmas advertising. There's no spectacle and no natural "missing note" moment.

## C. GROUND CONTROL

Grandad and Grandma watched the space station pass every Christmas Eve. This year Nell works out when it will pass (with Claude) and plans to show him. Clouds threaten. The whole street switches off its lights so the sky is dark enough. The clouds part, and the station glides over.

**Strengths:** it's beautiful and quiet, and the "street turns its lights off for one person" image is lovely.
**Risks:** Claude computing the pass time is too close to *Claude solves it*. An old man, a telescope and the Moon sits squarely in John Lewis's *Man on the Moon* (2015) territory. The payoff is a dot moving across a dark sky, which is subtle on a phone screen.

**Decision: keep SEEN FROM SPACE.** Neither alternative comes close. I've borrowed one idea from C: the *dark street* becomes a deliberate gesture, because at the dead switch every neighbour has switched their own lights off so his house can be the brightest thing on Earth.

## Challenges to the lead concept (and what changed)

1. **The loft hatch breaks the ladder rule.** A loft means a ladder, and Nell never climbs ladders. That's a hard rule, and it's also the very rule she types to Claude at the Turn. → **The boxes live in the garden shed.** She spots the shed key on its hook, tagged "SHED" in Grandma's handwriting. That's safer, more visual in the snow, and keeps her a rule-follower, so the Turn line is earned.
2. **The photo should do more work.** → **The photo is staged so the climax can rhyme with it:** young Grandma points at the sky and laughs, while young Grandad crouches by the front door with a bulb between his fingers. At the climax Grandad kneels by the same door with a bulb, and after the blaze Nell points at the sky exactly as Grandma did. Grandad sees it, and that's what breaks him open into his first laugh. The camera then rises along her pointing arm, so the pull-out to space is motivated.
3. **The misdirect needs a double meaning.** → "how many lights do you need to be seen from space" reads, mid-film, as *a kid who wants Santa's sleigh to spot her house*. Her wall shows roof plans, a chimney and an X, which are exactly what a Santa trap would look like.
4. **The reveal needs a picture, not just a sentence.** → After the readable reply ("…Who lives on his street?"), the camera pulls back from her wall. Every string on it leads to the middle: a child's drawing of Grandad's house ablaze, with a stick-Grandma pointing up. The trap was never a trap.
5. **The ladder man's nod.** → He reads the drawing, looks across at Grandad alone in the dark window, and shuts the door in her face. Nell deflates. A beat, then the door reopens and the end of a very long ladder pokes out first. He nods once. That's the laugh and the lump in one move.
6. **The dead switch should be musical.** → Grandad's fingers walk the string bulb by bulb, and each bulb is a note of the motif on solo piano. His fingers stop at the bulb where the missing note should be. Nell twists it, and the note and the blaze land on the same frame.
7. **The coda should close the opening image.** → Instead of Nell's bedroom, the coda returns to **the empty armchair from shot A4**. It isn't empty any more: Nell is asleep in it under Grandma's cardigan, with the tablet on her lap, Grandad beside her and the window ablaze. I log this as a deliberate departure from the brief because it is stronger.
8. **The bell.** It's planted silent and frosted by Grandad's door in the opener, and it tinkles after the blaze.
